01

What you already have

Two numbers matter, and your operation already produces both of them.

The recommendation. CropManage takes evapotranspiration data from the state's CIMIS weather stations, runs it against your crop, your soil and your planting date, and tells you how long to run. That's University of California research, and it's the same science the state respects.

The applied. What actually ran. Your crew knows this — the hours, the block, the date. It goes into CropManage's Applied Hours field.

That's the whole water side, and it isn't broken. Every report described in this document is built out of those two numbers. Nothing needs to be replaced.

The one soft spot

The applied number only helps if it actually gets recorded. An irrigation event with no hours against it isn't a zero — it's a hole, and at year end it becomes a number somebody has to invent. That's the single most common failure, and it's not a technology problem.

02

Does this even land on you?

Before anything else — find out whether you have a state filing obligation at all. Plenty of ranches don't, and the ones that do often don't know it.

Three things decide it:

  • Your basin's priority. High and medium priority basins are managed under the groundwater law. Low and very low are not.
  • Whether your basin is in good standing. If your local agency's plan is accepted, you report to them. If your basin has been put on probation, or has no local agency at all, you also report directly to the State Water Board.
  • How much you pump. Volume thresholds decide whether you need a certified meter, and in some basins whether you're excluded entirely.
The exemption, and why it probably isn't you

Someone pumping two acre-feet a year or less, for household use only, is exempt from reporting and fees. That's a house well. A commercial ranch is not going to qualify.

To confirm for your ranch

Which subbasin you sit in, which agency covers it, and that agency's current standing with the state. That's one phone call, and it determines everything on the following pages.

Part 205 of 10

05

The calendar

The one that catches everybody

The reporting year is the water year — October 1 through September 30. Not the calendar year. Not your crop season. If your totals are added up January to December they will not match what you file, and the mismatch is the kind of thing that draws a second look.

Beyond that, the dates split into two kinds, and only one of them is the same for everybody.

Fixed

  • The water year boundary — September 30
  • State filings cover the preceding water year

Varies by agency

  • When your local agency wants its report
  • When allocation statements are issued, and when a carryover claim has to be made
  • Metering deadlines, which have been set basin by basin
To confirm for your ranch

Your agency's filing date and its allocation-statement date. We'll put your actual dates on this page rather than a general calendar — a deadline that isn't yours is worse than no deadline at all.

Part 206 of 10

06

Hours don't count

Your crew records hours. No agency accepts hours. Everything has to arrive in acre-feet, attributed to a well.

The arithmetic is simple:

1,200 GPM × 60 min = 72,000 gal/hr
72,000 ÷ 325,851 gal per acre-foot = 0.221 acre-feet per hour

8.5 hours on that well = 1.88 acre-feet

The arithmetic isn't the problem. The problem is the two inputs, because most ranches have never written either one down:

  • The flow rate of each well or block. Somebody knows it. It's rarely on paper.
  • Which wells serve which fields — including fields fed by two wells, and how that gets split.
Why this one matters more than it looks

Whoever holds the well-to-field map is the only one who can turn field records into a well-level filing. Without it, no amount of good irrigation data produces a report. With it, the report is arithmetic.

Part 207 of 10

07

What it costs to be late, short, or wrong

If your basin is on probation

Fees go directly to the state: $300 per well, plus roughly $20 per acre-foot pumped in a probationary basin — about $25 in an area with no local agency at all.

If you go over your allocation

This is set by your local agency and it varies a great deal. Allocations are usually tiered — a base amount at no charge, more at a fee, and a penalty band above that. One valley agency's penalty pumping runs in the range of $310 to $500 an acre-foot. Yours may be structured completely differently.

The part people miss

Going over doesn't only cost cash. In many agencies the overage is subtracted from next year's allocation. You pay for it twice — once at the penalty rate, and again in water you won't have.

It runs the other way too

Water you don't use can often be carried into next year as credit, and in some places traded. A documented acre-foot you saved is an asset. That only works if the record is complete enough to prove it.

To confirm for your ranch

Your agency's tier structure, rates, cap, and penalty schedule. These numbers are real examples from real agencies — they are not a forecast of your bill.

Gather these six things

None of this requires a decision or a purchase. Most of it already exists — in somebody's head, in a drawer, or on a whiteboard in the shop.

  • 01Which subbasin and which agency One line. Everything else keys off it.
  • 02Last year's filing or allocation statement Whatever was actually submitted or received — it shows the real format and the real dates.
  • 03Your wells Count, capacity in gallons per minute, metered or not, and how extraction is measured today.
  • 04Your fields Names, acres, and parcel numbers.
  • 05Which wells serve which fields Including splits. A photo of the whiteboard is fine.
  • 06Flow rate per block or irrigation system However it's recorded. Without this, hours never become acre-feet.
Items 1 and 2 do most of the work

One allocation statement tells us your tier structure, your rates, your cap, your carryover rule and your deadline. It answers more than a month of research.

What good looks like at year end

You can judge your own records against this list without buying anything. If you can tick all six, you're in good shape whoever asks.

  • Every irrigation event has an applied number. No blanks, no "about."
  • Every event is tied to a well, not only to a block.
  • Totals roll up in acre-feet, by month, on the water year — October through September.
  • You can see who entered what, and when, and corrections are visible rather than overwritten.
  • Gaps are flagged, not filled in silently. A missing reading should be visible as missing.
  • The totals you file match the records behind them — and you can show the working if asked.
Why the fourth one matters

A number is only worth what you can defend. If a filing is questioned a year later, the answer that ends the conversation is a record showing who entered it, when, and what it replaced.

What we know, what we don't

Settled, and the same everywhere

  • Basins must reach balance by 2040, or 2042 depending on how overdrafted they are.
  • The exemption is 2 acre-feet a year, household use only.
  • State fees in a probationary basin: $300 per well plus a per-acre-foot charge.
  • The reporting year runs October 1 to September 30.
  • Surface water diversions above 10 acre-feet a year must be measured and reported.

Changes from agency to agency

  • Your allocation, its tiers, and whether there's a hard cap
  • The penalty rate for going over
  • Carryover and transfer rules
  • Filing dates and formats
  • Metering thresholds
