Part I

The Mandate

California made it illegal to keep over-pumping groundwater. Everything downstream of that — the budgets, the meters, the filings, the penalties — follows from one law and the deadline it set.

01

Why CropClient exists

The thesis

Every grower in a high- or medium-priority basin is being moved onto a metered water budget, and someone has to prove — with numbers, every year, to a state agency — that they stayed inside it.

CropManage, the UC Cooperative Extension irrigation system, is the system of record. It computes what a field should use from real evapotranspiration data, and it holds what was actually applied. CropClient is the field-side layer that captures the real reading at the valve and writes it back into CropManage's Applied Hours — so the record is complete, current, and defensible instead of half-filled from memory at the end of the week.

The mandate creates the paperwork. CropManage has the science and the system of record. CropClient makes sure the actuals actually get in there.

The vision

CropClient is the grower's complete budget, actual, and reporting system — with history, feedback, and follow-up.

02

SGMA — said accurately

The law, the deadlines, the teeth

Language discipline

There is no single statewide blanket "cut your water by X%" mandate for California farms. Anyone who says there is loses credibility with a grower in about ten seconds, because the grower knows better. What exists is a water-budget and sustainability mandate: SGMA requires local agencies to bring basins into balance by 2040 or 2042, and that local balancing forces the cuts. The obligation lands on the GSA; the GSA passes it to the grower as an allocation. Always say "water budget" and "allocation" — never "statewide water reduction mandate."

What it is

The Sustainable Groundwater Management Act, enacted in 2014, is the first law in California history to regulate groundwater. Before SGMA, if you owned the land, you pumped what you wanted.

  • The state designates basins by priority — high, medium, low, very low
  • High- and medium-priority basins must form a Groundwater Sustainability Agency
  • Each GSA writes a Groundwater Sustainability Plan showing how the basin reaches balance
  • The Department of Water Resources reviews the plans
  • If a local plan fails, the State Water Resources Control Board takes over
The deadlines that matter
MilestoneDate
Plans due — critically overdrafted basinsJanuary 31, 2020
Plans due — other high and medium priority basinsJanuary 31, 2022
Sustainability achieved — critically overdrafted basins2040
Sustainability achieved — other high and medium basins2042

2040 is not a soft target. It's the date by which pumping must actually be in balance, and GSAs are stepping allocations down on interim milestones to get there. The cuts are happening now, not in 2039. First real pumping limits began landing in 2026.

Scale: 21 basins in critical overdraft, 8 of them in the San Joaquin Valley. 261 GSAs formed across roughly 140 basins; 116 plans submitted, about 85% approved or conditionally approved as of 2024.

Teeth — probation

When DWR finds a plan inadequate, the State Water Board can designate the basin probationary. That's the moment the state, not the local agency, starts collecting from individual growers:

  • $300 per well, plus roughly $20 per acre-foot pumped in probationary basins — up to about $25/AF in unmanaged areas
  • Extractors above a volume threshold must install certified flow meters
  • Monthly extraction tracking, filed annually through the GEARS portal
  • If deficiencies aren't cured within a year, the Board can impose an interim management plan — the state running the basin directly

Litigation: the Kings County Farm Bureau sued the State Water Board, and a Kings County Superior Court judge issued a preliminary injunction delaying penalties and fees on Tulare Lake growers. Whether the state can charge individual farmers directly is live and unresolved.

2026 dates in play: initial extraction reports due to the Board February 1, 2026; reports covering July 15, 2024 – September 30, 2025 due May 1, 2026. In April 2026 the GEARS portal was reported buckling under load — one irrigation district GSA got only 4 of 50 landowner uploads through.

The portal failure is the opportunity in miniature

The state built a form. Nobody built the system that produces the number that goes in it.

De minimis

Under SGMA statute, a de minimis extractor — 2 acre-feet a year or less, domestic use only — is exempt from reporting and fees, but should still notify the Board through GEARS to stop compliance notices. Individual probationary orders have used different thresholds for exclusion, so check per basin — don't hard-code 2 AF.

03

The reporting — this is the product

The requirement everything else serves

Everything else in this briefing is context. This section is the requirement. If CropClient produces these outputs correctly, from a record the grower trusts, the product works.

Reporting comes in two tiers, and a grower can owe both at once.

Tier 1 — to your GSA, in every managed basin

The water-budget machinery, run locally at parcel or farm-unit precision:

  • An allocation issued per parcel or farm unit
  • Pumping reported against it
  • Carryover credits — SGMA expressly lets a GSA allow unused allocation to carry into the next year
  • Voluntary transfers — allocation traded between growers where the GSA permits
  • Overdraft penalties — one published example: $500 per acre-foot over allocation, and next year's allocation reduced by the exact overage
  • Growers receive annual statements of their water account

Read that penalty structure twice

An acre-foot you can't account for costs $500 and is subtracted from next year. Conversely, an acre-foot you save and can prove becomes a carryover credit — an asset. Accurate records pay in both directions. That's the economic argument, and it doesn't depend on anyone caring about regulation for its own sake.

Tier 2 — to the State Water Board, via GEARS

Required for extractors in probationary basins and unmanaged areas. Filed annually for the preceding water year through the Groundwater Extraction Annual Reporting System.

Measurement rules
  • Volumes measured by a device or method satisfactory to the State Water Board
  • In the Tule Subbasin: measurement from January 1, 2025; extractors above 500 AF/yr need certified flow meters — or a Board-approved alternative — from March 1, 2025
  • The Board has approved evapotranspiration-based methods as an alternative for tracking some large extractions — which is CropManage's native domain
  • Readouts in acre-feet, cubic feet, or gallons; meters installed, maintained, operated, inspected and monitored for accuracy

The core engineering problem

The two systems do not speak the same units.

The mandate's unit:   acre-feet   per WELL      per MONTH,   tied to a place of use
CropManage's unit:    hours       per PLANTING  per EVENT,   tied to a field

Bridging those is the central technical work of this project. At minimum it requires:

  1. Hours → volume — flow rate per irrigation system or block, applied to run time
  2. Planting → well — which wells served which planting; a many-to-many map
  3. Event → month — rolled up by water year, not calendar year
  4. Field → place of use — parcel and APN mapping for the GEARS record

Item 2 is the one that matters most. CropManage is organized around ranches and plantings; GEARS is organized around wells. Whoever holds the well-to-planting map is the only party who can produce the report.

What a finished filing needs to survive

  • Who entered the number and when — updatedBy, lastUpdatedDate, already in our records
  • Recommendation versus actual — mgrHours against appliedHours
  • The measurement method used for each well
  • An audit trail of corrections, not silent overwrites
  • Gaps flagged rather than hidden — a missing entry is a known hole, not a zero
04

The rest of the compliance load

Same records, different agencies

SGMA is the headline, but a Central Coast or Central Valley grower carries more than one reporting obligation — and each is a hook for the same underlying field data.

Surface water — SB 88 (2015)

Anyone diverting more than 10 acre-feet a year of surface water must measure and report the timing, rate, and volume of diversions to the State Water Board. Accuracy within ±10–15% depending on the size of the right. Monthly recording, annual electronic filing. Phase-in began January 1, 2017 for rights of 1,000 AF/yr and above; large diverters publish daily telemetered data updated at least weekly.

Nitrogen — the Irrigated Lands Regulatory Program

Administered by the Regional Water Quality Control Boards. Growers file:

  • Total Nitrogen Applied — nitrogen from fertilizer, organic amendments, compost, and irrigation water
  • Irrigation & Nitrogen Management Plan summary — applied against removed at harvest
  • In high vulnerability areas for nitrate leaching the plan must be certified — by a certified professional, or by grower self-certification through CDFA's training and exam

Why this matters to us

Nitrogen applied through irrigation water is a reportable line item. Water records and nitrogen records are the same records, and CropManage already computes both. That's a second mandate served off one dataset.

Agricultural Water Management Plans

Water suppliers serving more than 25,000 irrigated acres must adopt and submit a plan to DWR — which pushes measurement and reporting demands down onto their grower customers. That's a channel: the district needs the data, and the grower has to produce it.

05

What it costs the industry

PPIC, San Joaquin Valley

  • Farm water availability drops ~3.15 million acre-feet by 2040 — about 20% of current supply. SGMA alone accounts for 2.7 maf.
  • ~500,000 acres must come out of fully irrigated production even under optimistic supply-augmentation scenarios.
  • Without new supply or water trading: ~900,000 acres fallowed.
  • Economic hit: $4.5 billion in farm GDP and 50,000 jobs.

The read: every acre-foot a grower can document, defend, or avoid wasting has direct dollar value. Water is moving from a fixed cost of doing business to a traded, metered, penalized input. Software that measurably reduces applied water while producing the compliance record is selling into a market being created by law.

Part II

The Systems

What UC built, what we build on top of it, and the exact seam between the two.

06

CropManage

The science layer

A free, web-based irrigation and nitrogen management decision support tool from UC Cooperative Extension / UC ANR, developed by Michael Cahn and funded by CDFA's Fertilizer Research and Education Program. First released in 2011 for lettuce on the Central Coast, since expanded to many crops and into the Central Valley.

How it computes
  • Crop water need from CIMIS evapotranspiration data
  • Multi-year local research models for crop growth and nitrogen uptake
  • Soil nitrate quick test to adjust the fertilizer recommendation
  • Outputs: when to irrigate, how long to run, how much nitrogen to apply
Structure

Grower creates a Ranch → adds Fields / Plantings → confirms soil type → associates weather stations and water sources → logs irrigation events.

Crops and geography

Strawberries, raspberries, broccoli, cauliflower, cabbage, celery, spinach, bell peppers, cilantro, and head, romaine, leaf and baby lettuce. Salinas Valley and Central Coast in origin, expanding into the San Joaquin Valley and Tulare Lake Basin. Note that the Salinas Valley 180/400-foot aquifer is itself critically overdrafted, with seawater intrusion more than five miles inland — CropManage's home turf is under the same mandate as the Valley.

The API we consume

Register at cropmanage.ucanr.edu, then authenticate with username and password to receive an access token. v2 methods are prefixed /v2/; UC ANR recommends v2 over older methods being phased out. Our code also calls /v3/.

07

Where CropClient fits

The seam, corrected at the source

CropManage already tracks actual water usage. It is not recommendation-only. Growers record usage in the field and it goes into the Applied Hours field on CropManage's online data entry form. CropManage holds both legs — recommended and applied. CropClient's job is field-side capture and write-back. 

1. CropManage  →  recommended hours       (ET/CIMIS-driven, per planting — CropManage's output)
2. CropClient  →  create_next_irrigation  (next event from last record + interval;
                                           recommended hours land in Manager Hours)
3. CropClient  →  read_meter              (finds the LAST record for the ranch/planting,
                                           selects it, prompts the Applied Hours field —
                                           a person types the number; no device is read)
4. CropClient  →  update_record           (writes mgrHours + appliedHours, status = -1)
5. CropClient  →  sync                    (batch all status = -1 → POST new / PUT existing
                                           into CropManage's Applied Hours via /v3/)

Our grid mirrors CropManage's data entry form field for field — scheduledDate, interval, mgrHours, appliedHours — which is why the sync is a straight write, not a translation layer.

Where the value is

The mandate needs a complete, current record of applied water. CropManage has the right field for it. The friction is getting the number in there — today that means someone remembers the reading and types it into a web form back at the office, later, from memory or a scrap of paper.

  • Capture at the point of work — read_meter puts the right record on screen with the field waiting; nothing to search for, nothing to remember later
  • Manager Hours against Applied Hours, side by side — planned versus real, visible immediately
  • Batch sync, so field entry is offline-tolerant and reconciles later
  • Natural language via ClientAI, so a field worker talks instead of navigating forms

A record that's actually complete is what makes the compliance report possible. Incomplete Applied Hours is the difference between a defensible filing and a guess.

The third leg

Beyond what CropManage holds, the water company or district bill is a third number:

Recommended (CropManage)  →  what the science said the field needed
Applied     (CropManage)  →  what the meter says went on        ← CropClient puts it here
Billed      (district)    →  what was delivered and charged     ← not in CropManage

Reconciling billed against applied is how a grower catches a leaking valve, a miscalibrated meter, or a wrong invoice. That leg sits outside CropManage and is CropClient's own differentiator — its data source is still an open question.

Competitive landscape

  • Groundwater Accounting Platform — state-supported, open-source, built with DWR and the California Water Data Consortium; pilots at Rosedale–Rio Bravo, Yolo County FC&ID, Merced Irrigation-Urban GSA, Pajaro Valley WMA. Aimed at GSAs, not growers.
  • GSA Water Dashboard — commercial SGMA platform for agencies: allocation tracking, pumping reports, compliance workflow.
  • AgWaterAI — sensors, water budgets, SGMA reports.

The positioning gap

The funded, mature tools are built for the agency — the regulator's side of the meter. CropClient is on the grower's side, and it's the only one sitting on top of UC's actual agronomic recommendation engine. The agency's dashboard tells you what you owe. CropClient tells you what to do about it, and proves you did.

The worked example

320 acres · 6 fields · 3 wells · Water Year 2026

Rancho Del Sol is fictional. The farm, its wells, and every figure below are invented to illustrate the process. The rules are real and sourced, but rates and thresholds vary by basin and by GSA — verify against your own agency before relying on any number here.

Stage 1 — The budget, and the second budget nobody talks about

Rancho Del Sol farms 320 irrigated acres of cool-season vegetables in the Salinas Valley, inside the 180/400-Foot Aquifer subbasin. Their GSA issues an annual allocation per parcel: 2.0 acre-feet per acre for water year 2026, plus 12 acre-feet of unused credit carried in from last year. That's the budget they're allowed.

There's a second budget, and it's the one that governs whether the crop lives: what CropManage says the planned rotation will actually need. Those two numbers are set by different authorities, and nothing forces them to agree.

Allocation issued by GSA2.0 AF/acre × 320 acres640.0 AF
Carryover credit from WY2025unused allocation, carried forward under GSA accounting rules+ 12.0 AF
Water available to farm652.0 AF
Crop demand for the planned rotationCropManage ET model · romaine, broccoli, spinach, double-cropped690.0 AF
Gap between plan and allocation− 38.0 AF

This is the feedback moment

The plan is over budget by 38 acre-feet before a single valve opens. A grower who learns this in March can change a rotation, shift a planting, or buy a transfer. A grower who learns it from a penalty notice in December can only pay. Nothing in the current process surfaces this gap early — that's the opening.

Stage 2 — The actual: hours in the field, acre-feet on the filing

Through the season CropManage issues the recommended hours for each event — that's its output, computed from ET. Those land in the record as Manager Hours, the plan. Against them sits Applied Hours, what actually ran, entered by the irrigator on a phone standing at the valve.

Then the translation problem: the farm records hours; the state accepts only acre-feet, attributed to a well.

The Gonzales problem

Gonzales draws from two wells. To file, every acre-foot applied there has to be split between Well B and Well C — and the split has to be defensible. Whoever holds this mapping is the only party who can produce a well-level report from field-level records. If CropManage doesn't hold it, CropClient does.

The filing is monthly, per well, for the water year — October 1 through September 30. Not the calendar year, and not the crop season. Every rollup has to respect that boundary or the totals won't match the filing.

Open questions

Ordered by what blocks the product

Blocking — these gate the reporting product
  1. Does CropManage hold a well ↔ planting mapping? GEARS reports per well; CropManage is organized per planting. If the link doesn't exist upstream, CropClient owns it. Highest-value unknown in the project — answer it first.
  2. Where does hours → acre-feet conversion come from? Flow rate per block or irrigation system. Without it there is no report, because the state does not accept hours.
  3. Does CropManage expose a water-budget or applied-water report? It holds the data. Whether it produces a season-total, per-field, acre-feet output a GSA will accept is unconfirmed.
Important — these shape the product
  1. What does the target GSA actually require? Allocation basis, carryover rules, transfer rules, penalty schedule, statement format, filing deadline. These vary by GSA and are the real spec for Tier 1 output.
  2. Will a GSA accept a grower-generated report, or must everything go through the agency's own portal? Determines whether we produce a filing or a defensible worksheet behind one.
  3. Which basin do we target first? Salinas Valley — CropManage's installed base, seawater intrusion urgency — versus Tulare Lake and Tule, where probation, state fees and GEARS filing make the pain maximal.
  4. Where does water-company billing data come from? API, CSV, PDF, or manual entry?
  5. Per-basin de minimis and exclusion thresholds — they vary by order.
Worth confirming
  1. Production API host — dev versus prod cutover for CropManage.
  2. Is CropManage's ET method itself a Board-approved measurement alternative? If it qualifies, the science tool becomes an accepted measurement device — an extraordinarily strong position.
