01

The budget — and the second budget nobody talks about

October · start of the water year

Rancho Del Sol farms 320 irrigated acres of cool-season vegetables in the Salinas Valley, inside the 180/400-Foot Aquifer subbasin — critically overdrafted since 2014, with seawater intrusion more than five miles inland. Their Groundwater Sustainability Agency issues an annual allocation per parcel.

For water year 2026 they're allocated 2.0 acre-feet per acre, and they carry in 12 acre-feet of unused credit from last year. That's the budget they're allowed.

But there's a second budget, and it's the one that governs whether the crop lives: what CropManage says the planned rotation will actually need, computed from CIMIS evapotranspiration data against their planting schedule. Those two numbers are set by completely different authorities, and nothing forces them to agree.

This is the feedback moment

The plan is over budget by 38 acre-feet before a single valve opens. A grower who learns this in March can change a rotation, shift a planting, or buy a transfer. A grower who learns it from a penalty notice in December can only pay. Nothing about the current process surfaces this gap early — that's the opening.

02

The actual — hours in the field, acre-feet on the filing

All season · every irrigation event

Through the season, CropManage issues the recommended hours for each event — that's its output, computed from ET. Those recommended hours land in the record as Manager Hours, the plan. Against them sits Applied Hours, what actually ran, entered by the irrigator on a phone standing at the valve.

Then comes the translation problem. The farm records hours. The state accepts only acre-feet, and it wants them attributed to a well, not a field.

Well A · 1,200 GPM × 60 min = 72,000 gal/hr ÷ 325,851 gal/AF = 0.2210 AF per hour

The Gonzales problem

Gonzales draws from two wells. To file, every acre-foot applied there has to be split between Well B and Well C — and that split has to be defensible. Whoever holds this mapping is the only party who can produce a well-level report from field-level records. If CropManage doesn't hold it, CropClient must.

Rolled up the way the state wants it

The filing is monthly, per well, for the water year — October 1 through September 30. Not the calendar year, and not the crop season. Every rollup in the system has to respect that boundary or the totals won't match the filing.

The financial — water keeps a balance sheet

September 30 · the books close

This is the part growers feel. An allocation is an opening balance. Every irrigation event is a debit. Unused water is an asset that carries forward; overdrawn water is a liability priced by the acre-foot. It is double-entry accounting with a crop attached.

The swing

$6,000 in cash and 18 acre-feet of allocation, on a 320-acre farm, in one year — decided entirely by whether anyone noticed the gap in time. That argument works on a grower who doesn't care about regulators at all, which is most of them.

04

The reports — what actually has to be filed

October onward · the obligation

There is no single form. A grower can owe all of the following in the same year, to different agencies, on different deadlines, in different units — assembled from the same underlying irrigation records.

To the GSA — every managed basin

The routine, local obligation. Parcel-level accounting of what was allocated against what was pumped, plus the credit machinery: carryover balances, voluntary transfers between growers, and penalties. GSAs track this at parcel precision and issue growers an annual statement.

To the State Water Board via GEARS — probationary basins and unmanaged areas

Filed annually for the preceding water year through the Groundwater Extraction Annual Reporting System. This is the one with hard field requirements:

Volumes must be measured by a method satisfactory to the State Water Board. In probationary basins, extractors above the volume threshold need certified flow meters — or a Board-approved alternative — and meters must be installed, maintained, inspected and monitored for accuracy, reading in acre-feet, cubic feet, or gallons.

Worth chasing down

The Board has approved evapotranspiration-based methods as an accepted measurement alternative for some large extractions. ET is precisely what CropManage computes. If that modeling qualifies, the science tool becomes an accepted measurement method — and CropClient sits directly on top of it. Unverified, and worth a call to the Board and to UC ANR.

Also owed, from the same records

Two more filings draw on the identical dataset, which is why one well-kept record serves several obligations:

  • Nitrogen — Total Nitrogen Applied and INMP summary, to the Regional Water Quality Control Board. Nitrogen delivered in irrigation water is a reportable line item, so water records and nitrogen records are the same records. In high-vulnerability areas the plan must be certified.
  • Surface water diversions, if Rancho Del Sol takes more than 10 acre-feet a year from the Salinas River. Monthly recording, annual electronic filing, measurement accurate to within ±10–15%.
